Overview

In this assignment we implement a simple rasterizer for a simplified version of SVG files. Starting with drawing single-color triangles, we then use a small grid of blocks to speed up rasterization. Next we add supersampling to make the triangle edges smoother. And also implement under block sampling. Another interesting part is transforms: we use homogeneous coordinates for translation, scaling, and rotation, and add keyboard controls to rotate the whole view. We also use barycentric coordinates to interpolate colors and texture coordinates across triangles. In the end we implement nearest and bilinear texture sampling, then add mipmap level sampling to reduce aliasing. Together these parts give us a vector graphics renderer that can display simplified SVG files.

Task 1: Drawing Single-Color Triangles

I begin rasterizing each triangle by computing its axis-aligned bounding box. I then clamp the bounds to the dimensions of the framebuffer so that only pixels within the visible region are considered.

To accelerate rasterization, I divide the bounding box into 4-by-4 pixel blocks. For each block, I evaluate its four corners against the triangle's three edge functions. Because a triangle is convex, if all four corners lie inside the triangle, the entire block is inside it; I can therefore fill every pixel in the block without performing an additional inside-triangle test for each one. However, four corners lying outside the triangle does not necessarily mean that the whole block is outside, since the block may contain the triangle. A block can be safely rejected only when all four of its corners lie outside the same triangle edge. Any block that is neither fully accepted nor safely rejected is treated as a boundary block, and I test its pixels individually.

Compared with testing every pixel in the bounding box directly, this approach can accept or reject many pixels at once and thus avoid redundant edge-function evaluations. The block-level tests add some overhead and use stricter conditions for early acceptance or rejection, but they should improve performance when rasterizing scenes with many triangles, especially when large regions of their bounding boxes are clearly inside or outside the triangles.

Task 2: Antialiasing by Supersampling

For supersampling, I split every pixel into a uniform grid. The spacing between adjacent samples is \(1 / sqrt(sample\_rate)\), and each sample is placed at the center of its subpixel cell. I apply the same three edge-function tests to every subpixel sample. Covered samples are written into sample_buffer using the pixel offset \((y \times width + x) \times sample\_rate\) plus the local subpixel index \(sub\_y \times grid\_size + sub\_x\); uncovered samples retain the background color.

To support supersampling, our sample buffer is extended to host more value. When the sample buffer is resolved, the subpixel colors belonging to each output pixel are averaged. A pixel that is only partially covered by the triangle therefore becomes a blend of the triangle and background colors instead of being classified as entirely covered or uncovered. This produces intermediate colors along triangle boundaries and reduces the jagged stair-step artifacts visible with one sample per pixel. Increasing the sample rate gives a more accurate estimate of pixel coverage and smoother edges, as shown below, at the cost of proportionally more coverage tests, storage, and resolve work.

Task 4: Barycentric Coordinates

Barycentric coordinates express a point relative to the three vertices of a triangle rather than relative to the global x- and y-axes. This makes them especially useful in rasterization: they describe not only where a sample lies inside a triangle, but also how strongly each vertex contributes to that sample.

For a triangle with vertices A, B, and C, any point P in the same plane can be written as \(P = \alpha A + \beta B + \gamma C\), where \(\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1\). The values \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), and \(\gamma\) are the barycentric coordinates of P. A point is inside the triangle when all three weights are nonnegative. Each weight is 1 at its corresponding vertex and decreases linearly to 0 along the opposite edge, so a larger weight means that the point is more strongly influenced by that vertex.

The same weights can interpolate any per-vertex attribute, including color, brightness, depth, texture coordinates, and normals. If the three vertex colors are CA, CB, and CC, the color at P is \(C(P) = \alpha C_A + \beta C_B + \gamma C_C\). In Figure 4.1, the vertices are pure red, green, and blue, so the interpolated color is directly determined by the three barycentric weights. Points near the red vertex contain more red, points near the green or blue vertices are dominated by those colors, and points along an edge blend only the colors at that edge's endpoints. Near the center, the three weights are similar, producing a balanced mixture of all three colors. Since the weights vary linearly across the triangle, the result is a smooth color gradient with no discontinuities.
